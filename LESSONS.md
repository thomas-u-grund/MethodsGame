# The Secret of the Lost Codebook — Lessons learned, act by act

Each act of the game ends on a summary slide: a recap card, *"Act … : what you learned"*, one lesson
per room and a closing thought, read over by the narrator's closing line. This file collects them in one
place, word for word, for players and for instructors who use the game in a methods course.

The whole game is one small research project: **Do first-years who attend more Methods lectures get
better exam results? (This winter term)**

---

## Act I — The Question

*Folder stamp: QUESTION EXISTS · "Act I · The Question: what you learned"*

- A topic is not a question. Check how a number is built; then say who,
  what and when, and leave the direction open.
- Long words are not an explanation.
- One black swan is enough to refute "all swans are white". Found, not made.
- X may cause Y, Y may cause X, a third thing may cause both, or it may be
  chance. A correlation never tells you which.

> *A good question is precise, can be answered, and does not know its answer yet.*

**Narrator:** Four rooms, four lessons. You turned a topic into a question that evidence can answer.
That is Chapter One.

---

## Act II — Theory

*Folder stamp: THEORY EXISTS · "Act II · Theory: what you learned"*

- Don't blindly trust the AI. Read the whole paper, and check that it exists.
- A mechanism says *how*, in your own words. Famous names are not an argument.
- A small claim that could fail beats a huge one that never can.
- Write down, before you look, which result would prove you wrong.

> *Theory means different things to different people. In the end it is a way of making sense of the
> world — and one with a clear mechanism can be tested, which makes it the better kind.*

**Narrator:** Four boxes, four lessons. You wrote down how you could be wrong, and sealed it. That is Chapter Two.

---

## Act III — Data

*Folder stamp: QUESTION HAS DATA · "Act III · Data: what you learned"*

- People agree knowing what to, can walk away, and cannot be named.
- One question asks one thing, and does not lean.
- A sample is best drawn at random (later statistics rely on it), from a list of exactly the people you mean.
- Chase the people who did not answer. Never swap in whoever is nearest.

> *Good data can be collected or found. Either way, know how it came to be: who was asked, what,
> whether they were chosen at random, and who is missing.*

**Narrator:** Every number now has a history: who agreed, what was asked, who was drawn, who answered. That is Chapter Three.

---

## Act IV — Evidence

*Folder stamp: RESULT EXISTS · "Act IV · Evidence: what you learned"*

- Check every line the machine writes. The analysis is still yours.
- Run the test you promised. Pull the lever often enough and something
  always pays out.
- Say what the result means at its true size, and name its limits.
- The finding: go to the lectures. They help most where they make you practise: a modest effect, and a real one.

> *Be solid. Do the work, one step after another.*

**Narrator:** The data would have said yes to anything. You made it say only what it could. That is Chapter Four.

---

## Act V — The Annual Meeting

*Folder stamp: SUBMITTED · "Act V · The Annual Meeting: what you learned"*

- A prediction only counts if it was made before the result.
- Your contribution is what you add to what was known, in words the data can carry.
- Answer the question you were asked, in one sentence.
- Design, scope, interval, literature. Evidence answers a claim; volume does not.

> *There was never a Codebook. There was only Methods.*

**Narrator:** A small poster, a true one, and a hall that finally listened. That is Chapter Five.

---

## The ending — Reviewer 2

The paper comes back with **REVISE AND RESUBMIT** and forty-seven comments from Reviewer 2. The game ends
there, on comment one: answering them is Part Two, *The Revisions* (the companion game, in planning).

Small and true outlasts large.
